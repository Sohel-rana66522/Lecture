Part A — Water Use and Quality Requirements

Q1. Explain why water quality is described as "a function of land use in the catchment area." (3 marks)

Water quality results from both natural phenomena and human activity. Even a catchment area kept in a fully natural state (forest/woodland) is not free of impurities — rainfall runoff and infiltration pick up suspended solids (soil particles) and dissolved solids (ions from dissolved rock) purely through contact with the soil, so soil protection and composition already influence quality. Human interference then adds to this in two forms: concentrated discharge (domestic or industrial wastewater released at a point) and diffused discharge (fertilizers/pesticides applied over land). Because both natural runoff and human land-use practices ultimately determine what enters the water, the overall quality of a water body is fundamentally shaped by how the land in its catchment is used and occupied.

Q2. List the main uses of water, and explain the general relationship between water use and required water quality. (4 marks)

Main uses of water: domestic supply, industrial supply, irrigation, animal supply, preservation of aquatic life, recreation and leisure, breeding of aquatic species (aquaculture), generation of electricity, navigation, landscape harmony, and dilution/transport of wastes.

There is a direct relationship between the use and the quality demanded: domestic water supply is the most demanding use, requiring the satisfaction of multiple quality criteria (freedom from harmful chemicals and organisms, low hardness/aggressiveness, aesthetic acceptability). Only domestic and industrial supply are routinely associated with prior treatment, because of their strict quality requirements. At the other extreme, simple dilution and transport of wastes is the least demanding use, with no specific quality requirement. In practice, a single water body is often assigned multiple uses simultaneously (e.g., a reservoir used for supply, power generation, recreation and irrigation together), so it must then satisfy the combined requirements of all of them.

Q3. Describe the internal cycle of water use, from raw water to receiving body. (4 marks)

Besides the natural hydrological cycle, water passes through an "internal" cycle in which it stays liquid but its quality is progressively modified at each stage:

  • Raw water — water abstracted from a river, lake or aquifer, at its initial quality.
  • Treated water — raw water transformed by treatment so it complies with its intended use (e.g., public or industrial supply).
  • Raw wastewater — water that, after use, has undergone further quality changes and become a liquid waste.
  • Treated wastewater — wastewater treated to remove its main pollutants before discharge to the receiving body.
  • Stormwater — rainwater that picks up pollutants while flowing over the ground, collected by stormwater systems before discharge.
  • Receiving body — the water body that receives both stormwater and treated-wastewater effluent, where quality is modified once more through dilution and self-purification. Managing this internal cycle — planning, designing, constructing and controlling the works needed to keep water at the quality its intended use requires — is a core role of environmental engineering.

Q4. Discuss how quality requirements differ across different water uses, with examples. (5 marks)

Quality requirements vary sharply by use:

  • Domestic supply requires freedom from health-harmful chemicals and organisms, low hardness/aggressiveness, and aesthetic acceptability (low turbidity, colour, taste, odour).
  • Industrial supply requirements depend on how the water contacts the product: water incorporated into a food/drink/medicine product needs the same health-based purity as domestic supply, water contacting the product needs quality "variable with the product," while water not contacting the product (e.g., boiler or cooling water) mainly needs low hardness and low aggressiveness (to prevent scaling/corrosion).
  • Irrigation needs vary by crop: water for horticulture or raw-eaten produce must be free of health-harmful substances and have non-excessive salinity, while irrigation for other plantations mainly needs freedom from substances harmful to soil/plants.
  • Animal water supply requires freedom from substances/organisms harmful to animal health.
  • Aquaculture needs freedom from chemicals/organisms harmful to animals, workers and consumers, plus adequate nutrients.
  • Recreation differs by contact type: primary contact (swimming, water-skiing) demands health-safe water with low suspended solids/oil-and-grease, while secondary contact (boating, fishing, viewing) only needs a pleasant appearance.
  • Energy generation needs low aggressiveness (hydroelectric) or low hardness (thermal/nuclear cooling towers).
  • Transport mainly needs low levels of coarse material that could endanger vessels. This range illustrates why a single "water quality" number is meaningless without specifying the intended use.

Part B — Analysis and Testing of Water

Q5. What are the aims of water testing, and what are the three broad categories of tests used to determine water quality? (4 marks)

The aims of laboratory testing are: to determine the quality of raw water; to determine what treatment processes are required; to ensure treatment is being properly carried out at each stage; and to confirm that the treated water conforms to standards before supply. The three broad categories of test are:

  • Physical tests — indicate the aesthetic quality and treatment-unit performance (temperature, colour, odour, turbidity).
  • Chemical tests — measure dissolved substances and the extent of pollution (pH, TDS, hardness, chloride, nitrate/nitrite, iron/manganese, heavy metals).
  • Bacteriological tests — indicate the presence of pollution-causing bacteria and whether the water is safe to drink.

Q6. Describe the physical and chemical tests used to determine water quality. (5 marks)

Physical tests evaluate visible/sensory characteristics: temperature (affects aquatic life and chemical reaction rates), colour (an unusual colour may indicate contamination), odour (unpleasant smells suggest pollution or bacterial activity), and turbidity (cloudiness caused by suspended particles).

Chemical tests measure dissolved substances: pH (indicates acidity/alkalinity); Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) (concentration of dissolved minerals and salts); hardness (caused mainly by calcium and magnesium — temporary hardness from their carbonates/bicarbonates, permanent hardness from their chlorides/sulfates); chloride (naturally low, but rises in contaminated water, making it salty and corrosive to pipelines); nitrate and nitrite (harmful in excess, especially to infants); iron and manganese (cause staining and affect taste); and heavy metals such as lead, arsenic, mercury and cadmium (toxic contaminants).

Q7. Describe how bacteriological examination of water is carried out, including the total count test and the three stages of the E. coli test. (6 marks)

Bacteriological tests detect harmful (pathogenic) bacteria, which cause diseases such as typhoid, cholera and dysentery. Since testing for every pathogen individually is impractical, water is instead tested for E. coli, a harmless coliform bacterium discharged in large numbers in faeces — its presence indicates the possible presence of pathogens too.

  • Total Count Test: a water sample with agar added is incubated (20 °C for 48 hours, or 37 °C for 24 hours); bacteria grow into visible, countable colonies. The total count should not exceed 100 per cc of water.
  • E. coli (coliform) test, done in three stages:
    1. Presumptive test — water samples in sterile tubes with lactose are incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours; gas formation indicates a positive result (bacteria present); if no gas forms, the sample is re-checked at 48 hours.
    2. Confirmed test — a positive sample's lactose broth is transferred either to a green lactose bile fermentation tube (gas after 48 hours confirms the water unsafe) or plated onto endo/eosin-methylene-blue agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours (visible colonies confirm a positive result).
    3. Completed test — the sample is added to both lactose broth fermentation tubes and agar tubes and incubated 24–48 hours at 37 °C; gas formation confirms the water unsafe for drinking, and further tests identify the bacteria type; no gas formation means the water is safe.

These standards exist because each parameter maps onto a specific risk — aesthetic (colour, odour, taste), health (heavy metals, nitrate, bacteria), or infrastructure (hardness/chloride causing scaling and corrosion) — so together they define what makes water safe and acceptable for its intended use.

Part C — Water Pollution: Definition and Sources

Q9. Define water pollution, and describe its four main sources of contamination. (3 marks)

Water pollution is the contamination of water bodies (lakes, rivers, oceans, aquifers, reservoirs, groundwater) that negatively affects how they can be used, usually caused by human activity. It arises from contaminants mixing with these water bodies, coming from four main sources: sewage discharges, industrial activities, agricultural activities, and urban runoff (including stormwater). It may affect either surface water or groundwater, and leads to degraded aquatic ecosystems, the spread of waterborne disease, and a reduced ability of the water resource to provide ecosystem services such as drinking water.

Q10. Distinguish between point and dispersed (nonpoint) sources of water pollution, and explain why one is easier to control than the other. (4 marks)

A point source is a discrete pipe or channel — such as an industrial facility's discharge or a city sewerage outfall — through which pollutants enter a water body from one identifiable location. A dispersed (nonpoint) source is a broad, unconfined area from which pollutants enter a water body diffusely, such as agricultural runoff, or urban stormwater drainage (which can carry sand/grit, automotive petroleum residues, and road de-icing chemicals) entering streams or lakes at many scattered points.

Point sources are easier to control because the contaminated water is already collected and conveyed to a single location, where it can be captured and treated. Dispersed sources are much harder to control precisely because they enter the environment at countless locations rather than one — despite significant progress in building modern sewage treatment plants (which mainly address point sources), dispersed sources continue to cause a large share of water pollution problems.

Part D — Types and Classification of Water Pollution

Q11. Describe the major types of water pollution based on where and how contamination occurs (groundwater, surface water, oil spillage, nutrient, and suspended-matter pollution). (6 marks)

  • Groundwater pollution: aquifers, replenished by rainwater, can absorb pesticides and fertilizers seeping through soil, and leaking septic tanks/sewage systems can introduce untreated waste directly into groundwater.
  • Surface water pollution: contamination of oceans, lakes and rivers occurring naturally (floods/tsunamis picking up fertilizers, pesticides, debris), accidentally (oil spills, agricultural runoff), or intentionally (industries dumping waste directly into waterways).
  • Oil spillage: occurs from accidental spills, transport, runoff, or intentional dumping; extremely difficult to remove — just one litre of oil can pollute one million litres of water. Oil spills make up roughly 12% of oil entering the oceans (the rest from shipping/transport, draining and dumping). Oil spreads quickly over the surface, blocking oxygen and sunlight penetration (preventing photosynthesis), suffocating fish, and smothering seabird feathers, exposing them to oil ingestion.
  • Nutrient pollution: nutrients such as calcium, chloride, fluoride, magnesium, potassium and sodium are normally present in drinking water, but excess amounts (e.g., from fertilizer runoff) can block sunlight and stunt underwater organism growth, disrupting the ecosystem.
  • Suspended matter pollution: arising from soil erosion, runoff, discharges, stirred bottom sediments and algae blooms; settling particles damage underwater life and leach chemicals, while floating particles block oxygen and sunlight penetration.

Q12. Describe chemical, microbiological, thermal, and oxygen-depletion (algal bloom) pollution. (5 marks)

  • Chemical pollution: the most common form of water pollution; pesticides/fertilizers, heavy metals and industrial solvents are major contributors. Contaminants reaching drinking water can include arsenic, fracking fluids, lead, chlorinated disinfection by-products, PFAS, uranium, nitrogen, and bleach.
  • Microbiological pollution: bacteria, viruses and protozoa (from human/animal waste, sediments, aquatic vegetation) can cause life-threatening disease if not properly decontaminated; some developing/low-income countries lack adequate treatment systems to filter such contamination.
  • Thermal pollution: the degradation of water quality caused by changes in water temperature, from both human activity and natural occurrences.
  • Oxygen depletion pollution (algal blooms): certain algae reproduce rapidly, forming dense concentrations that consume large amounts of dissolved oxygen, harming other aquatic life; some algae species also release toxins during blooms, which endanger human/animal health via ingestion (causing gastrointestinal problems, liver damage, neurological effects). Controlling nutrient pollution — the primary cause of algal blooms — is therefore key to protecting water quality and public health.

Q13. Classify water pollution into physical, chemical, and biological categories, giving examples and effects of each. (6 marks)

  • Physical water pollution — changes the physical properties of water (appearance, temperature, clarity). Examples: suspended soil/sediments, plastic waste/litter, industrial solid waste, thermal pollution (hot water from power plants/industries). Effects: reduces water transparency, blocks sunlight needed by aquatic plants, increases temperature (reducing dissolved oxygen), and harms aquatic habitats.
  • Chemical water pollution — occurs when harmful chemicals enter water bodies. Examples: pesticides/herbicides, nitrate/phosphate fertilizers, heavy metals (lead, mercury, cadmium), industrial chemicals/acids, oil spills/petroleum products, detergents and household chemicals. Effects: toxic to aquatic organisms, contaminates drinking water, causes human health problems (poisoning, cancer), and leads to eutrophication (excessive algal growth).
  • Biological water pollution — caused by living organisms or biological material. Examples: bacteria (e.g., E. coli), viruses, protozoa, parasitic worms, sewage/animal waste, harmful algal blooms. Effects: spreads waterborne diseases (cholera, typhoid, dysentery), makes water unsafe for drinking/recreation, and can reduce oxygen levels, harming aquatic life.

Part E — Effects of Water Pollution

Q14. Discuss the effects of water pollution on land. (4 marks)

Water pollution degrades adjacent land by contaminating soil, reducing agricultural yields, and accumulating toxic heavy metals in the food chain; polluted irrigation water introduces harmful pathogens and chemicals into the earth, threatening ecosystems and human health. Specific effects:

  • Soil contamination and fertility loss — toxic wastewater/chemical runoff seeps into the ground, altering soil chemistry and destroying essential microorganisms, stunting plant growth.
  • Decreased agricultural productivity — irrigation from contaminated rivers/lakes causes poor crop yields and compromised crop quality.
  • Food chain disruption — hazardous substances (lead, cadmium) transfer from polluted water to crops and livestock and eventually humans, causing long-term health risks through biomagnification.
  • Vegetation destruction — acidic or oil-heavy runoff poisons riparian vegetation, stripping ground cover and accelerating topsoil erosion.
  • Wastewater risks — using untreated wastewater in livestock farming introduces infectious pathogens into soil and the food supply.

Q15. Discuss the effects of water pollution on groundwater, including its public health and socioeconomic consequences. (5 marks)

Water pollution threatens groundwater aquifers by letting hazardous surface contaminants (heavy metals, pathogens, agricultural chemicals) seep into underground sources; once polluted, groundwater is very difficult and costly to clean.

  • Public health: pathogen-laden groundwater causes acute waterborne diseases (cholera, dysentery, hepatitis); heavy metals (arsenic, lead) and industrial chemicals raise the risk of cancer, nerve damage and developmental issues — arsenic contamination is a major concern in densely populated delta regions like Bangladesh; high nitrate levels (from fertilizers) cause blue baby syndrome (methemoglobinemia) in infants.
  • Ecological/agricultural damage: polluted groundwater pumped for irrigation accumulates toxic compounds in soil, stunting crop growth and reducing food safety; contaminated groundwater eventually discharges into lakes/wetlands/rivers, introducing persistent toxins that threaten aquatic biodiversity.
  • Socioeconomic repercussions: as water tables drop from over-extraction, pollutant concentration rises, forcing communities/industries to find costly alternative clean-water sources; remediation of polluted aquifers requires extensive, long-term, financially burdensome interventions.

Q16. Discuss the effects of water pollution on surface water. (5 marks)

Water pollution degrades surface water quality, disrupts ecosystems, and threatens public health via toxic chemicals, raw sewage and agricultural runoff.

  • Ecosystem/wildlife degradation: nutrient pollution (nitrogen/phosphorus) triggers eutrophication — toxic algal blooms that block sunlight and consume dissolved oxygen as they decompose, creating aquatic "dead zones"; toxic chemicals/heavy metals poison fish, amphibians and birds, disrupting food chains and causing population decline; bioaccumulation (mercury, microplastics) concentrates toxins as they move up the food chain to apex predators and humans.
  • Human health risks: contaminated surface water carrying untreated sewage/animal waste spreads pathogenic bacteria/viruses/parasites, causing typhoid, cholera and hepatitis; industrial wastewater and mining runoff introduce toxic chemicals leading to acute poisoning, chronic organ damage and increased cancer risk.
  • Agricultural/economic repercussions: irrigating with polluted surface water introduces toxins into crops, reducing yields and endangering food safety; decontaminating heavily polluted surface water for drinking/commercial use requires massive municipal treatment investment.

Q17. Discuss the effects of water pollution on aquatic life. (4 marks)

Water pollution devastates aquatic ecosystems by introducing toxic chemicals, depleting dissolved oxygen and altering water chemistry, causing rapid die-offs and disrupted reproduction.

  • Oxygen depletion/dead zones — nitrogen/phosphorus runoff triggers rapid algae growth; decomposing algae consume vast amounts of dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic dead zones where fish/invertebrates suffocate.
  • Toxicity and bioaccumulation — heavy metals (mercury) and pesticides accumulate in aquatic organisms and magnify up the food chain, impairing neural function, immune systems and reproductive success in predators.
  • Physical harm/habitat damage — oil spills coat animals, destroying insulation in birds/mammals and causing gill damage/fin rot in fish; marine plastic debris causes lethal entanglement and fatal digestive-tract obstructions.
  • Disrupted chemical balances — pollutants alter pH and temperature, making the environment hostile to native species and favouring invasive, opportunistic ones.

Q18. Discuss how water pollution affects the sea, giving examples of the different pollutant categories involved. (5 marks)

Up to 80% of sea pollution originates from land-based runoff and sewage; it destroys habitats, contaminates food webs, depletes oxygen and introduces toxic chemicals.

  • Plastics/microplastics — over 100,000 marine mammals and countless fish die annually from ingesting plastic or entanglement in discarded fishing gear; microplastics accumulate up the food chain, posing risks to seafood consumers.
  • Nutrient pollution (eutrophication) — agricultural fertilizer runoff triggers explosive algal blooms; decomposing algae consume oxygen, creating suffocating dead zones.
  • Oil spills — surface slicks coat seabird feathers and marine mammal fur, stripping insulation and ability to swim/fly; ingested crude oil causes reproductive problems, gill damage and mass die-offs.
  • Chemical and wastewater pollution — untreated wastewater and industrial chemicals introduce viruses/bacteria causing diseases (e.g., white pox) in coral reefs and shellfish; heavy metals like mercury poison marine life at all trophic levels, changing food web structures.

Q19. Describe how water pollution disrupts ecological systems, using the fertilizer-in-a-lake example to illustrate the cascading effect. (5 marks)

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms (plants, animals, micro-organisms) interacting with each other and their physical environment (water, air, soil); water pollution seriously disrupts this balance through:

  1. Loss of biodiversity — pollutants kill sensitive species; some become endangered or disappear locally.
  2. Disruption of food chains — toxic substances accumulate in organisms and move up the chain, with higher-level predators receiving the highest concentrations.
  3. Eutrophication — excess nutrients cause rapid algal growth; blooms block sunlight and, on decomposing, reduce oxygen, killing fish and other organisms.
  4. Habitat destruction — pollution damages wetlands, rivers, lakes and coral reefs, so organisms lose breeding, feeding and shelter areas.
  5. Reduced water quality — changes in pH, temperature and chemical composition stress or kill organisms.
  6. Bioaccumulation and biomagnification — toxins like mercury and pesticides accumulate in organisms and increase in concentration up the food chain.
  7. Reduced ecosystem services — healthy ecosystems provide clean water, food, flood control and climate regulation; pollution reduces this capacity.

Cascading example: when fertilizers enter a lake — (1) algae grow rapidly → (2) oxygen levels decrease → (3) fish die from lack of oxygen → (4) birds and animals dependent on fish lose their food source → (5) the entire ecosystem becomes imbalanced. This demonstrates how a single pollutant input propagates through an entire ecological web rather than staying contained to one species or process.

Q21. Explain why the leather and tannery industry's contribution to water pollution is considered especially serious compared with other industries. (4 marks)

The tannery industry releases chromium (a heavy metal directly toxic to aquatic life and, in its hexavalent form, a known carcinogen) and sulfides (both oxygen-demanding and toxic, and a source of highly hazardous H₂S gas), alongside other toxic chemicals used in beamhouse and tanning operations. What makes this contribution especially serious, compared with a purely organic pollutant load, is that these are persistent, non-biodegradable toxic substances: unlike organic BOD, which biological treatment can break down, chromium does not degrade — it either has to be physically/chemically removed and recovered, or it accumulates in sludge, soil and the food chain. The note specifically highlights that tannery discharge "can contaminate both surface water and groundwater," meaning poor management risks polluting drinking-water aquifers as well as rivers — a dual pathway of harm that is less pronounced for industries whose main pollutant is simple organic matter (e.g., food processing).
