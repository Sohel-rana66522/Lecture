Q1. What is in-plant pollution management? Mention its key elements. (3 marks)

In-plant pollution management is a range of practices designed to minimise the environmental impact of industrial operations at the source, before waste ever leaves the plant. Its key elements are: maintaining proper housekeeping; segregating waste streams; reducing water consumption; optimising chemical use; promoting recycling and reuse of materials; and monitoring the overall water footprint of the facility. Supporting elements include proper chemical storage, tight process control (temperature, pH, time, dosage), raw-material optimisation, equipment maintenance, energy management, and worker training.

Q2. Discuss the benefits of in-plant pollution reduction. (4 marks)

  • Reduced environmental impact — less pollution discharge protects natural resources and improves air/water quality.
  • Cost savings — efficient practices cut energy, water and waste-disposal costs.
  • Conservation of resources — more efficient use of water, chemicals, energy and raw materials.
  • Improved regulatory compliance — avoids penalties and legal issues.
  • Recovery of valuable materials — useful chemicals can be recovered and reused (e.g., chromium).
  • Improved workplace safety — proper handling/storage/housekeeping reduces hazards.
  • Enhanced brand reputation — sustainability-focused companies attract customers and build a better image.

Q3. Define housekeeping in an industrial context, and state its primary objectives. (3 marks)

Housekeeping is the systematic care and management of equipment, materials and workplace practices to ensure efficiency, safety and pollution prevention — more than simple cleaning, it is about discipline, preventive action and process integrity that reduces waste generation and environmental impact. Its primary objectives are to prevent losses of raw materials, water, chemicals and energy through leaks, spills or poor practice; to minimise pollution risk; to improve worker safety and occupational health; and to improve equipment reliability, reduce downtime, and ensure regulatory compliance — which together improve productivity and cut operating costs.

Q4. Describe the key elements of a good housekeeping programme in a tannery. (5 marks)

  • Material handling and storage — chemicals stored in dedicated, well-ventilated, clearly marked areas with spill-proof flooring and secondary containment (bunds, trays, double-walled tanks); hazardous substances labelled with GHS symbols and accompanied by accessible MSDS sheets; FIFO/FEFO stock rotation to avoid waste from expired chemicals.
  • Leak and spill management — drip trays and splash guards under pumps, tanks and valves; automatic shut-off valves and double seals on hazardous pipelines; spill kits (absorbent pads, sand, sawdust, activated carbon) kept at strategic points; workers trained to respond immediately, since small leaks accumulate into large losses over time.
  • Equipment maintenance — preventive maintenance (lubrication, calibration, cleaning, inspection) plus predictive techniques (vibration analysis, infrared thermography, ultrasonic testing) to catch problems, e.g. a small pipe crack, before they cause a leak or hazard.
  • Workplace cleanliness — clean floors and organised storage reduce accidents/contamination; a shift from high-water-use cleaning to high-pressure/low-volume systems, mechanical sweepers and vacuum-based cleaning cuts both water use and wastewater generation; colour-coded bins and floor markings separate hazardous from non-hazardous waste.
  • Worker training and awareness — regular training on chemical handling, storage, emergency response and waste minimisation; posters/signage for reinforcement; incentive programmes for reporting unsafe practices, so housekeeping becomes a shared responsibility rather than a task for a few individuals.

Q5. With practical examples, explain how simple housekeeping measures reduce pollution in a tannery. (3 marks)

In tanneries specifically, installing drip trays under drums and segregating chrome-laden waste streams significantly cuts chromium contamination of the general wastewater — a very low-cost intervention with a large pollution benefit. More broadly (by analogy, as the note illustrates from other industries), regular preventive maintenance of pumps and pipelines prevents solvent losses and VOC emissions in chemical plants, and FIFO storage with routine cleaning prevents material spoilage and microbial contamination in food processing. These examples show that housekeeping is not a peripheral "cleanliness" activity but a direct, low-cost pollution-prevention tool.

Q6. What is waste segregation, and why is it important? (4 marks)

Waste segregation means classifying different types of waste into their correct categories at the point of generation, rather than mixing them, so each stream can be managed, treated, recycled or disposed of appropriately. It is important because it: protects the environment by preventing hazardous materials from contaminating soil, water and air; enables resource recovery, since clean recyclable/compostable streams can be efficiently collected and reused; reduces landfill volume and the associated greenhouse-gas generation; improves recycling efficiency, since uncontaminated waste streams give higher-quality recycled material; protects health and safety by limiting public exposure to hazardous substances; and gives cost savings, since segregated waste is cheaper to process than mixed waste requiring extensive sorting.

Q7. Mention the major categories of waste that should be segregated in an industrial facility. (3 marks)

  • Organic waste — food/garden and other biodegradable material, suited to composting.
  • Recyclable waste — paper, cardboard, plastic, glass, metals (rinsed/cleaned before recycling).
  • Non-recyclable waste — used tissues, plastic wraps and similar non-biodegradable scraps, sent to general waste.
  • Hazardous waste — chemicals, fluorescent bulbs, medical waste, used batteries — requiring dedicated hazardous-waste facilities.
  • E-waste — discarded phones, computers, batteries, containing harmful chemicals if not segregated.
  • Construction and demolition debris — wood, brick, concrete, often recyclable/reusable.
  • Medical waste — syringes, bandages, medicines, requiring clearly labelled, health-and-safety-compliant bins.
  • Miscellaneous waste — textiles, rubber and similar materials not fitting the above categories.

Q8. Why must chromium-laden waste streams be segregated, and what does the method of segregation depend on? (3 marks)

Chromium segregation isolates chromium from other waste components so that it can be properly treated, recovered/recycled, or safely disposed of — critical in industries like leather tanning and electroplating, where mixing chromium into the general effluent turns a recoverable raw material into a hazardous, difficult-to-treat waste. The choice of segregation method depends on the physical state of the waste stream (solid or liquid) and the valence state of the chromium present — the highly toxic and mobile hexavalent form, Cr⁶⁺, versus the far less toxic trivalent form, Cr³⁺.

Q9. Describe the chemical reduction and precipitation process used to treat hexavalent chromium in tannery wastewater. (5 marks)

This is a two-stage process for treating wastewater containing toxic Cr⁶⁺:

  1. Reduction — in an acidic environment (pH 2–3), a reducing agent such as sodium bisulfite or ferrous sulfate is dosed to convert hexavalent chromium (Cr⁶⁺) into the far less toxic trivalent form (Cr³⁺). Completion of this step can be monitored using an oxidation–reduction potential (ORP) sensor.
  2. Precipitation — the pH is then raised to an alkaline level (pH 8–10) using a base such as lime (calcium hydroxide) or caustic soda. This causes the now much less soluble Cr³⁺ to precipitate out as solid chromium hydroxide, Cr(OH)₃, which can be filtered out of the stream.

This sequence — acidic reduction first, then alkaline precipitation — is what allows recovered chromium to be separated from the water as a manageable solid rather than remaining a soluble, highly toxic contaminant.

Q10. Briefly describe other methods, besides chemical precipitation, used to segregate or recover chromium from liquid and solid waste streams. (5 marks)

Liquid streams:

  • Centralized chrome recovery — segregated chrome streams (especially in CETPs) are sent to a dedicated facility where chromium is recovered as a cake for regeneration and reuse.
  • Membrane filtration (reverse osmosis/nanofiltration) — semi-permeable membranes separate dissolved chromium ions, producing a chromium-rich concentrate (retentate) that can be reused.
  • Ion exchange — resin beads capture chromium ions, effective for low-concentration streams, though resins need periodic regeneration.
  • Bioelectrochemical systems — an advanced method using biological processes to convert toxic Cr⁶⁺ to non-toxic Cr³⁺ while simultaneously generating electricity.

Solid streams (e.g., leather shavings, spent catalysts):

  • Waste characterisation — analysing the waste first to determine the chromium form and other contaminants present, guiding the choice of treatment.
  • Physical separation — manually or automatically separating non-hazardous material from chromium-bearing material (e.g., separating chrome shavings from other organic tannery waste).
  • Hydrothermal separation — an innovative method that uses a mineraliser and heat/hydrothermal treatment to separate chromium from other components such as aluminium.
  • Incineration with control — removes organic content but risks oxidising harmless Cr³⁺ into the far more hazardous Cr⁶⁺ if not carefully controlled, requiring sophisticated equipment and careful ash handling.

Q11. Describe in-house (cleaner production) techniques used to reduce chemical use and pollution load at source in a tannery. (5 marks)

Cleaner production focuses on cutting waste and chemical use before effluent is even generated:

  • Advanced/high-exhaustion chrome management — can raise chrome uptake by the leather to as much as 98%, sharply cutting chromium in the waste float.
  • Hair-save liming — separates hair intact instead of dissolving it, reducing the chemical and organic load reaching the wastewater.
  • Low-ammonia or ammonia-free deliming/bating — alternative agents replace ammonium salts, cutting total nitrogen load in the effluent.
  • Enzymatic treatments — enzymes used in unhairing and bating reduce the need for harsh chemicals.
  • Segregation of wastewater streams — keeping chrome- and sulphide-bearing streams apart allows more targeted, efficient treatment and recycling.
  • Reuse of spent liquors — spent pickling and chrome-tanning liquors can be re-dosed (e.g., adding sulfuric/formic acid to spent pickle liquor, or basic chromium sulfate to used chrome liquor) and reused.

Q12. Describe the effluent treatment and reuse technologies used to recover chemicals and water after production. (5 marks)

Unlike in-house techniques, these act after the effluent has been generated, to recover and reuse chemicals/water:

  • Membrane filtration (NF/RO) — produces high-quality water for reuse by removing salts and hard-to-degrade organic compounds.
  • Membrane bioreactors (MBR) — combine biological treatment with membrane filtration for very high removal of organics and suspended solids, giving effluent quality suitable for recycling.
  • Electrochemical treatment (electrocoagulation, electro-oxidation) — effectively removes chromium and other pollutants; recovered chromium can sometimes be reused as a tanning agent.
  • Adsorption — low-cost adsorbents (e.g., coffee husks, clay, modified tannery waste) remove chromium, dyes and other pollutants.
  • Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), e.g. Fenton's oxidation — use highly reactive radicals to break down persistent, non-biodegradable organics, making the water more amenable to further biological treatment.
  • Coagulation/flocculation with dissolved air flotation (DAF) — physicochemically removes a high percentage of suspended solids, chromium and organic matter.

Two documented examples: chromium recovered via chemical precipitation or electrocoagulation (using copper electrodes) can produce a liquor suitable for reuse in tanning; and oxidising sulphide in unhairing effluent, followed by acidification, allows both sulphide reduction and protein recovery.

Q13. Define water footprint and describe its three components. (4 marks)

A water footprint is the total amount of freshwater consumed and polluted in producing goods/services used by an individual, community or organisation — accounting for both direct water use and the indirect water use spread across the entire production chain. It has three components:

  • Blue water footprint — surface and groundwater consumed during production (including animal husbandry and the tannery's own processes).
  • Green water footprint — rainwater consumed, mainly during growth of the animal's feed, plus water stored in soil that evaporates or transpires from plants.
  • Grey water footprint — the volume of freshwater theoretically needed to dilute and assimilate pollutants in the wastewater down to an acceptable water-quality standard.

Q14. Discuss the water footprint of the leather industry, including the stages that contribute most to it. (5 marks)

According to a 2023 Bangladesh study, the total water footprint of one ton of bovine crust leather is about 34,000 m³, with the largest share coming from the animal's life on the farm — the grey water footprint is particularly significant there because of pollution from both farming and processing. At the tannery itself, the wet processing stage accounts for 97% of the tannery's own water footprint. Within that, beamhouse operations (soaking and liming) are the largest contributors to both water consumption and pollution load, producing effluent high in organic matter, salt and other pollutants; post-tanning stages, especially dyeing, also contribute heavily, along with wet-back, rechroming and finishing. Overall, the tanning process itself uses an estimated 35–60 m³ (35,000–60,000 litres) of fresh water per ton of raw hide, most of it consumed in wet processing and released as highly polluting effluent.

Q15. Discuss the major environmental impacts of discharging untreated tannery effluent linked to its water footprint. (3 marks)

  • Contamination of water sources — untreated effluent carries high concentrations of hazardous chemicals (chromium, sulphides, salts, heavy metals), severely polluting local rivers, streams and groundwater.
  • Threat to aquatic life — the high pollutant load, including the low dissolved-oxygen conditions created by high BOD/COD, can be directly toxic to aquatic ecosystems.
  • Health risks — communities near tanneries, especially in developing countries, can suffer disease from exposure to contaminated water.
