01 Quality Circle

Q1. Define Quality Circle. 2+ definitions

A Quality Circle is a small group of employees from the same work area, doing similar work, who voluntarily meet regularly (about an hour a week) to identify, analyze, and resolve work-related problems, improving total performance and enriching work life.

  • Udupa (1986): a small group of employees in the same work area who voluntarily meet regularly to identify, analyze and resolve work-related problems.
  • Ishikawa: a small group that performs quality-control activities voluntarily within the same workshop, as part of company-wide QC activities, for self- and mutual development.

Q2. Write down the features of Quality Circle. exam favorite · 4–5 marks

  1. Small group of employees — ideally 8 to 10 members (below 5 loses vitality due to absenteeism; above 15 denies active participation to everyone).
  2. Same work area / similar work — a homogeneous group, not inter-departmental, so discussions are intelligible to all members.
  3. Voluntary — no compulsion to join or not join; based on the principle of voluntarism.
  4. Meets regularly — about one hour every week (3–4 times a month), during or after working hours as members decide.
  5. Identifies, analyzes and resolves work-related problems — stays focused on quality, productivity and performance, not grievances or demands.
  6. Leads to total performance — improves quality, productivity, cost, safety, giving tangible and intangible gains.
  7. Enriches work life — better working environment, job satisfaction, cohesive team culture, improved relationships.

Q3. Why is 8–10 considered the ideal size for a Quality Circle?

Fewer than 5 members causes the circle to lose vitality (high absenteeism can make it inactive); more than 15 members makes the number of interactions unmanageable and denies some members the opportunity for active participation.

Q4. What is the main focus of Quality Circle discussions, and what is explicitly excluded?

The focus is strictly "work-related problems" — quality, productivity, cost, safety. Extraneous issues such as grievances or demands are excluded.

02 Quality Function Deployment (QFD)

Q5. What is Quality Function Deployment (QFD)? 2024 exam · Q7b

QFD is a structured approach to defining customer needs/requirements and translating them into specific plans to produce products that meet those needs. It is built around capturing the "voice of the customer" (stated and unstated needs) and converting it into product requirements.

Q6. How is the "voice of the customer" captured?

Through direct discussion/interviews, surveys, focus groups, customer specifications, observation, warranty data, and field reports.

Q7. Discuss the main phases of QFD. 2024 exam · Q7b · 5 marks

QFD methodology runs through four basic phases across the product development process, each producing one or more matrices:

  1. Product Planning — translates customer needs ("whats") into product requirements/technical characteristics ("hows"), summarized in the House of Quality matrix.
  2. Product Design — translates product requirements into specific part/component characteristics and design targets.
  3. Process Design (Process Planning) — translates design characteristics into key process operations and parameters needed to produce the product.
  4. Process Control / Quality Control — translates process operations into production requirements: quality control plans, testing, equipment maintenance, and operator training.

Overall flow: Product Planning → Product Design → Process Design → Process/Quality Control.

Q8. What is House of Quality (HOQ)? Which components are essential to facilitate QFD? 2024 exam · Q7c · 3 marks

HOQ is the product-planning matrix used in the first phase of QFD to translate customer requirements ("whats") into technical/design requirements ("hows"). Essential components:

  • Customer requirements (the "Whats")
  • Technical/design requirements (the "Hows")
  • Relationship matrix (linking whats to hows)
  • Planning matrix / importance weighting of customer needs
  • Technical correlation matrix (the "roof" — interrelationships among the hows)
  • Competitive assessment / benchmarking
  • Technical priorities and targets (bottom row)

Q9. Why does QFD require multiple functional departments to be involved?

Because product development involves Marketing, Design Engineering, Quality Assurance, Manufacturing/Manufacturing Engineering, Test Engineering, Finance, and Product Support — QFD is a team-based, cross-functional communication tool, not just a set of matrices.

Q10. What is the real value of QFD beyond the matrices themselves?

The matrices are a communication tool, but the real value lies in the process of team communication and decision-making that QFD forces — leading to consensus, greater commitment, better coordination, and reduced downstream redesign time (more time spent up-front, less time spent fixing disagreements later).

03 Project Management (PM) Concept

Q11. Define "project" and "project management."

  • Project: a one-shot, time-limited, goal-directed major undertaking requiring varied skills and resources; a temporary endeavor with a defined start and end, undertaken to create a unique product or service.
  • Project management: the overall planning and coordination of a project from conception to completion, aimed at meeting stated requirements and completing on time, within cost, and to required quality standards.

Q12. List the steps/stages in Project Management.

  1. Project Definition and Scope
  2. Technical Design
  3. Financing
  4. Contracting
  5. Implementation
  6. Performance Monitoring

Q13. What are the four basic elements a Project Manager must manage simultaneously?

  1. Resources — people (right skills, right quantity, right time), equipment, and materials.
  2. Time/Schedule — breaking the project into tasks, estimating duration, sequencing them.
  3. Cost — estimated cost, actual cost, and variability/contingency (weather, suppliers, design allowances).
  4. Scope — what the project is meant to deliver.

All four are interrelated and must be managed together for project success.

Q14. Explain the 80/20 Rule in project management.

A small share (20%) of the work is vital while the rest (80%) is trivial. In practice, the first 10% and the last 10% of a project (20% of total work) consume 80% of the time and resources — so project managers should plan extra attention/buffer for the start and finish phases.

Q15. What are the elements of the Project Management Life Cycle?

  1. Need Identification
  2. Initiation
  3. Planning
  4. Executing
  5. Controlling
  6. Closing out

Q16. Briefly describe each phase of the Project Management Life Cycle.

  • Need Identification: projects identified internally (energy manager spotting opportunities/audits) or externally (audits by outside auditors); ranked by cost-effectiveness, sustainability of savings, ease of monitoring, technology availability, and environmental/social benefits.
  • Initiation: getting the project started; stakeholders agree on initiation; success depends on accurate information, management support, and authorization.
  • Planning: the most important phase — defines activities, products, schedule, cost, risk, quality, and staffing; produces the approved project plan.
  • Executing: the project plan is carried out; team focus shifts from planning to performing, observing and analyzing work; deliverables are produced.
  • Controlling: comparing actual vs. planned performance and taking corrective action when there are significant differences.
  • Closing out: performed once objectives are met and the customer accepts deliverables (or if cancelled/terminated early); includes contract closeout and administrative closure.

04 Business Process Reengineering (BPR)

Q17. Define BPR.

BPR (Hammer & Champy / Sherwood-Smith) is the fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in critical, contemporary measures of performance such as cost, quality, service, and speed — devising new ways of organizing tasks and people and redesigning IT systems so processes support organizational goals.

Q18. Define "business process" as used in BPR.

Hammer & Champy: a collection of activities that takes one or more kinds of input and creates an output of value to the customer — a specific ordering of work activities across time and space, with a beginning, an end, and clearly identified inputs/outputs.

Q19. Distinguish between BPR and TQM. high exam-relevance

AspectBPRTQM
Nature of changeMajor, discrete, radical changeMinor, continuous / incremental improvement
Time to resultsFaster — realized in 12–18 monthsSlower — can take several years
RiskFar riskier undertakingLower risk, steady gains
DirectionTop-down (driven by management)Bottom-up (involves staff at all levels)
ApproachComplete redesign of processesOngoing improvement of existing processes

Q20. Distinguish between BPR and DSS (Decision Support Systems).

Both focus on business processes and improving them through change, but BPR focuses on the whole organization, while DSS focuses on one individual decision at a time.

Q21. How are BPR and TQM related in practice?

Companies that implemented TQM before undertaking BPR faced less resistance to change (MacIntosh & Francis, 1997). This has led to the concept of "participative BPR," combining BPR's radical redesign with TQM's staff involvement to reduce resistance.

05 Taguchi's Approach to Quality

Q22. Define quality according to Taguchi.

Taguchi defines quality negatively: "Quality is the loss imparted to society from the time the product is shipped" (i.e., the amount of damage/loss incurred to society from the moment the product leaves the factory). "Society" includes both the customer and the company's own employees.

Q23. What is Taguchi's main objective for any quality improvement programme?

To minimize the variation of product performance about its target value — a product causes loss not only when it falls outside specification limits but whenever it deviates at all from the target value. Smaller performance variation means better quality.

Q24. Distinguish between the traditional (conventional) loss function and Taguchi's loss function.

  • Traditional view: all products within specification limits are considered equally good; all products outside are equally bad (a step-function view).
  • Taguchi's view: loss increases as performance deviates from the target value, even within specification limits — modeled as a continuous quadratic (parabolic) function, not a step function.

Q25. Write Taguchi's Loss Function and define its terms.

L(Y) = (M / D²) × (Y − t)²
  • L(Y) = quality loss function
  • t = target value (most desirable value of the product)
  • Y = actual performance/response observed in test or analysis
  • M = producer's loss (in monetary terms)
  • D = customer's tolerance

Q26. What do "t + D" and "t − D" represent in Taguchi's loss function graph?

They are the functional limits beyond which 50% of the product/system needs customer maintenance (i.e., the tolerance boundaries around the target value).

Q27. Why is Taguchi's approach significant for quality improvement programmes?

It provides a straightforward, well-integrated system for applying statistical experimental design, encourages closer collaboration between statisticians and engineers, increases statistical awareness in industry, and allows quality to be built in early — at the design/prototype stage, during maintenance, or during process installation — rather than inspected in afterward.
